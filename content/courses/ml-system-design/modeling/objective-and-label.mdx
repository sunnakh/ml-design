## 1.1 Business → ML objective

### The objective ladder

<Diagram src="modeling/objective-ladder.svg" />

Each rung down is **easier to measure** but **less faithful** to the goal. Every gap between two rungs is a place the system can go wrong. The optimizer only sees the bottom rung. Only the **A/B test** tells you whether improving the bottom rung actually moves the top one.

### Design choice 1: which target?

| Candidate target | Positives per 1k impressions | Feedback delay | Alignment with GMV |
|---|---|---|---|
| Click | ~30 | Seconds | Weak: clickbait wins |
| Add-to-cart | ~3 | Minutes | Medium |
| Purchase | ~0.5 | Hours to days | Strong |
| Purchase × price | ~0.5, heavy-tailed | Days (returns: weeks) | Strongest, but high variance |

*(Illustrative numbers, realistic orders of magnitude for e-commerce.)*

The pattern: **dense, fast signals are misaligned; aligned signals are sparse and slow.** There's no free option.

### Design choice 2: one blended label, or decomposed predictions?

**Blended:** one model on one label, such as "purchased". Simple, but sparse, and business priorities get baked into training.

**Decomposed** (the industry standard for feeds, ads and search): separate predictions combined at serving time:

```
score(u, i) = P(click | u, i)
            × P(purchase | click, u, i)
            × price(i)^α
            − λ · P(return | purchase, u, i)
```

Why decompose:

- **Denser data per model.** The purchase model trains only on clicks, so its positive rate is ~0.5/30 ≈ 1.7% instead of 0.05%.
- **Business weights live outside training.** α and λ are product decisions you can tune weekly without retraining.
- **Debuggable.** When GMV drops, you can tell which piece broke.

Costs: more models (or heads) to train, serve and monitor; errors multiply through the product; and **each P must be a calibrated probability**, not just a good ranking score (see 4.5).

### Assumptions

1. **The proxy correlates with the goal and keeps correlating after you optimize it.** Goodhart's law: once a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.
2. **Short-term signals predict long-term value.** A purchase today doesn't guarantee the user returns next month.
3. **Logged behavior reflects preference.** It really reflects what you *chose to show*. Users can't click what they never saw (see 4.4).

### Trade-offs

- **Density vs alignment**: see the table above.
- **Value weighting vs variance**: one $3,000 laptop can dominate the loss and push luxury items. Cap price, log-transform it, or tune α < 1.
- **Short-term vs long-term**: session GMV can rise while trust and retention fall.
- **Multiple stakeholders**: buyers, sellers and the platform. Stakeholders not in the objective become **guardrails**.

### Failure modes

| Failure | Symptom |
|---|---|
| Proxy hacking | CTR ↑, GMV flat or ↓. Clickbait thumbnails, bait prices. |
| Offline–online gap | Offline AUC +2%, A/B test flat. |
| Silent long-term damage | GMV ↑ for 2 weeks, retention ↓ after 2 months. Use a **long-term holdout** (e.g. 1% of users on the old system for months). |
| Outlier domination | The value-weighted model floods the feed with expensive items. |
| Guardrail breach | Return rate, complaints or seller concentration climb unnoticed. |

**Guardrail metrics** are defined together with the objective: return rate, p99 latency, seller diversity, complaint rate. The ship rule: the primary metric improves **and** no guardrail regresses.

### Connections

- **← Requirements:** the online metric and guardrails come from there.
- **→ 1.3 Labels:** your target decides what you must log.
- **→ Branch 3:** decomposition means several models or one multi-task model.
- **→ Branch 4:** each target needs its own loss; multiplying probabilities requires calibration.
- **→ Evaluation:** the A/B test is the only place you verify the link from ML target to business goal.
- **→ Monitoring:** track the proxy and the business metric side by side.
