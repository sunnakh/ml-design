## A1 Why evaluate

### The problem it solves

A model is fit to **past** data but used on **future** data, under conditions its own existence changes. Training loss only tells you how well the model fits examples it has already seen. A big enough model can memorize its training set and be useless on new users.

> **Evaluation is estimating how a decision will perform in the future deployment setting, using evidence you can get before (or while) you commit to it.**

1. **A decision.** You evaluate a choice: "ship the personalized model instead of bestsellers?" Evaluation is relative by default.
2. **The future deployment setting.** Next month's users, new products, holiday spikes.
3. **An estimate.** Every number has noise (variance) and can be off (bias). A number without uncertainty is half an answer.

### The three questions evaluation answers

| Question | Store example | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Is it better than what we have? | Beats bestsellers? | Relative (drives most decisions) |
| Is it good enough at all? | Latency under 50 ms, no offensive recommendations | Absolute (constraints) |
| Where and why does it fail? | Bad for new users? Recommends items already bought? | Diagnostic (drives the next iteration) |

### The core recipe

1. **Get data the model didn't learn from**, as close to deployment conditions as you can afford.
2. **Have the model make predictions** on it.
3. **Score the outputs** with a metric that reflects the value you care about.
4. **Compare against a baseline** and report the uncertainty.

Methods differ in how realistic step 1 is, which gives a **fidelity ladder**:

<Diagram src="evaluation/fidelity-ladder.svg" />

Training loss is coral because it isn't evaluation at all. Left is cheap, fast, safe and less realistic; right is costly, slow, risky and realistic. Use **the whole ladder as a funnel**: cheap methods filter out bad ideas, expensive ones confirm the survivors.

### The assumptions (where evaluation quietly breaks)

1. **Representativeness.** Test data from last spring won't represent holiday shoppers.
2. **Independence from training.** A feature computed over the whole log, including the test period, peeks at the answer.
3. **Correct labels.** "No click" might mean "didn't like it" or "never scrolled down".
4. **Metric validity.** Clicks rise with clickbait while purchases fall.
5. **Non-reactivity.** Old logs only contain reactions to bestsellers; you don't know reactions to items never shown.
6. **Fresh test set.** 200 experiments against one test set means you've partly fit to it.

### Design choices you make every time

- **What** to measure (A2), **against what** (A3), **on what data** (B1–B2), **where on the ladder** (C, E), and **how sure** you need to be (B7, C3).

### Trade-offs

- **Fidelity vs cost and speed.** A/B tests take weeks; offline evals take minutes.
- **Fidelity vs risk.** Higher fidelity exposes real users to a possibly worse model.
- **Reusing the test set vs trusting it.**

### Failure modes

- Reporting training or validation loss as "the model is good."
- No baseline, so "AUC 0.82" means nothing.
- One aggregate number hiding a group that got worse.
- A test set that doesn't match deployment.
- An offline win that is really leakage.
- Stopping at "better" without asking "how sure?"

### Connections

- **Requirements** defines the metric and success bar.
- **Data** produces splits and labels; data bugs become evaluation bugs.
- **Modeling** uses offline evaluation as its inner loop.
- **Deployment** is gated by evaluation.
- **Monitoring** is evaluation that never stops.

## A2 Metric ladder

### How it works

Each arrow means **"is assumed to predict."** Going down, metrics get faster, cheaper, less noisy and more sensitive. Going up, they get closer to real value.

<Diagram src="evaluation/metric-ladder.svg" />

| Level | Store metric | Speed | Used for |
|---|---|---|---|
| North star | 90-day revenue per user, retention | Months | Strategy, long-term holdouts (E3) |
| Online primary (OEC) | Purchases from the row per user | 1–3 weeks | **The** ship / no-ship metric in an A/B test |
| Online proxy | Row click-through rate (CTR) | Days | Early read, debugging |
| Offline | Recall@20 or NDCG@20 on next week's purchases | Minutes | Model selection and tuning |
| Training loss | Log loss of predicted click probability | Seconds | Gradient descent only |
| Guardrails | Latency, returns, diversity | Continuous | Veto a launch even if the primary wins |

**Every arrow is a hypothesis, not a fact.** "Better recall@20 → more purchases" must be checked empirically, and re-checked. Keep a log of past experiments:

| Experiment | Offline Δ recall@20 | Online Δ purchases/user |
|---|---|---|
| exp-12 | +3.1% | +1.2% |
| exp-15 | +0.4% | +0.1% |
| exp-19 | +5.0% | **−0.8%** |

If offline gains **usually move with** online gains, the offline metric is a trustworthy filter. When they disagree (exp-19), something broke in the chain (branch D).

### What makes a metric good

1. **Aligned (valid):** rises when real value rises.
2. **Sensitive:** moves detectably for a realistic change. 90-day revenue rarely moves significantly in 2 weeks.
3. **Hard to game.**
4. **Attributable:** your change moves it, not a marketing promotion.
5. **Cheap and fast enough** for the decision.
6. **Defined before the experiment.**

Alignment and sensitivity **trade off**, which is why the ladder exists.

### Design choices

- **Training label:** clicks (plentiful, noisy, gameable), add-to-cart, or purchase (sparse, closest to value). Often combined: `score = p(click) · w₁ + p(purchase) · price · w₂`. The weights are a business decision.
- **One OEC**, agreed beforehand. Several "primary" metrics means one always went up.
- **Guardrails and thresholds**, e.g. p99 latency +5 ms max, return rate +0.2 pp max. Hard vetoes.
- **Time horizon:** decide how to catch long-term harm (E3 holdouts).
- **Metric unit:** per user, session or impression (C3, gaming).

### Failure modes

- **Goodhart:** train on clicks and the model learns flashy "you won't believe the price" items. CTR +8%, purchases flat, returns up.
- **Ratio traps:** showing the row less often, only to engaged users, raises CTR while total clicks and purchases fall. Check numerator and denominator.
- **Misaligned ladder:** train on clicks, select on purchase recall, business wants margin.
- **Proxy decay:** the row moves below the fold and last year's aligned proxy stops being aligned.
- **Guardrail omission:** a heavier model adds 40 ms to the homepage and costs more sitewide than the row gained.
- **Choosing the metric after the fact.**

### Connections

- **Framing and requirements** build the ladder. **Data:** the training label is the bottom rung. **B3:** the offline rung in detail. **C2–C4:** OEC and guardrails become the A/B design. **D1:** when arrows break. **Monitoring:** the same ladder in production.

## A3 Baselines

### The problem it solves

"Test recall@20 = 0.21." Good? **You can't tell.** A metric only means something relative to reference points:

| Baseline | Question it answers | Store example |
|---|---|---|
| **Floor** (random, constant, base rate) | Is the pipeline working? | Random products |
| **Heuristic** | Is ML worth the complexity? | Popularity, "recently viewed" |
| **Simple ML** | Is the complex model worth its cost? | Matrix factorization, logistic regression |
| **Champion** (in production now) | **Should we switch?** The actual decision | Bestseller row |
| **Ceiling** (oracle, human) | How much headroom? Where's the bottleneck? | Oracle ranking the user's actual purchases first, within available candidates |

### How it works

Run **every baseline and the candidate through the same pipeline**: same test period, users, candidate set and metric code. Read the model's position **between floor and ceiling**:

<Diagram src="evaluation/baseline-ladder.svg" />

1. **Against the champion:** 0.21 vs 0.11 is +91% relative. The number the team will quote.
2. **Against the best cheap heuristic:** 0.21 vs 0.17 ("recently viewed") is +0.04 absolute. A one-line SQL heuristic gets **~80% of the gain** at almost no serving cost. Is the last +0.04 worth a feature pipeline, GPU serving, on-call and harder debugging?
3. **Against the ceiling:** headroom captured = (0.21 − 0.01) / (0.34 − 0.01) ≈ **61%**. At 0.33 you'd stop tuning the ranker and look at retrieval, which limits the ceiling.

### Assumptions

- **Equal effort:** baselines tuned seriously. **Equal conditions:** same data, split, candidates, filters, metric code. **Current numbers:** champion re-scored on today's test set. **Realistic ceiling:** the oracle respects system constraints.

### Design choices

- **Which baselines:** at minimum floor, strongest cheap heuristic, champion. Add simple ML for expensive candidates, a ceiling when deciding where to invest.
- **Tune baselines fairly.** If you'd be embarrassed to show its config to a reviewer, it's a straw man.
- **Report absolute and relative lift.**
- **Complexity-adjusted gain:** put gain next to latency, compute, maintenance, explainability.
- **Online baselines too:** sometimes run a heuristic arm (A/B/C).

### Trade-offs

- **Strong baselines make wins smaller but real**; weak ones make them big and fake.
- A day tuning the heuristic can save a quarter building a model that doesn't beat it.
- A heuristic that's 80% as good and never breaks can be the right choice.

### Failure modes

- **Straw-man baseline** (default hyperparameters, 10% of data).
- **Stale comparison** (champion's number from last year's report).
- **Mismatched conditions** (full catalog vs in-stock only).
- **No floor**, so pipeline bugs go unnoticed.
- **Beating the ceiling:** almost certainly **leakage or a metric bug** (B2).
- **Log bias** favoring popularity-like models (D2).

### Connections

- **A2:** same rung. **B1–B2:** same splits; leakage shows as "beating the ceiling". **B7 / C3:** is +0.04 bigger than noise? **C2:** the champion is the A/B control. **E1–E2:** launch gates. **Modeling:** build baselines before the model; sometimes the baseline is what you ship.
