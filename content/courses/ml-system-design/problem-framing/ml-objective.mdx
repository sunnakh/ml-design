Branch 1 defined business success (TCF). Branch 2 confirmed ML is worth building. Branch 3 asks: **what exactly will the model be trained to optimize, and how far is that from what we actually want?**

## 3.1 Proxy metrics

### What problem it solves

You can't train a model directly on TCF:
- It's **not differentiable**: a sum of dollars over decisions, not a smooth function of model weights.
- It's **delayed**: 120 days.
- It **depends on actions**: TCF changes with the policy, and the counterfactual is unobserved.

So you train on a **surrogate**: a target (what to predict), a loss (how to score errors) and weights (which examples matter more). Every surrogate is a **proxy**, and every proxy has gaps. Choose one whose gaps you understand and can close.

### How it works: the proxy chain

What you train is several steps removed from what you want. Each link has a specific gap:

<Diagram src="problem-framing/proxy-chain-with-gaps.svg" />

Three choices make up the ML objective.

**1. Target: what exactly to predict.**

| Option | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| P(any chargeback within 120 days) | Easy, lots of positives | Includes friendly fraud (2.2): a label gap |
| **P(true fraud)**: fraud-coded chargebacks + issuer alerts + confirmed review outcomes | Matches the checkout decision | Fewer positives, needs label engineering |
| Expected loss in dollars (regression) | Directly in dollars | High variance; a few huge orders dominate |

**ShopFast choice: P(true fraud).** The amount is applied later, in the policy layer (1.4). Keep the model's job narrow.

**2. Loss: how errors are scored.**

| Loss | What it gives | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Log loss** | Calibrated probabilities | When a cost policy consumes p (ShopFast's case) |
| Class-weighted or focal loss | Focus on the rare class / hard examples | Can help ranking, but **breaks calibration**. Recalibrate afterwards. |
| Pairwise / ranking loss | Good ordering | Top-K problems, e.g. a fixed-capacity review queue |
| Amount-weighted log loss | Aligns training with dollars | Higher variance; output is no longer a plain probability |

**3. Sampling and weighting.** Fraud is 0.2% of orders, so teams often keep all fraud and a sample of legit orders (e.g. 5%) to train faster. That **inflates the model's probabilities**, so you must correct them:

> If legit orders were kept at rate r, then **p_true = r · p_s / (r · p_s + 1 − p_s)**, where p_s is the score from the sampled model.

### Choosing a proxy: four tests

1. **Decision-aligned**: it outputs what the policy needs. ShopFast needs a *calibrated* p.
2. **Observable in time**: labels arrive within a usable window (2.3).
3. **Correlated with the goal in the operating region**: in the top 1–3% of scores, not just on average (1.3).
4. **Hard to game**: optimizing it hard doesn't break the link to the goal (the whole of 3.2).

### Assumptions

- Proxy and goal **move together** where you operate.
- That relationship **survives optimization pressure** (3.2).
- Label noise is **random-ish**, not tied to some segment.

### Design choices

- **Narrow target + policy layer** vs **end-to-end dollar target**. Narrow is easier to debug and reuse; end-to-end is theoretically more aligned but fragile.
- **One model for all fraud** vs **separate models per fraud type** (stolen card, account takeover, friendly fraud). Separate models give cleaner targets but multiply maintenance.
- **Loss choice, driven by the consumer**: a cost policy needs log loss; a capacity-limited queue can use a ranking loss.

### Trade-offs

- **Clean vs broad label**: less noise but fewer positives, versus more data with more noise.
- **Calibration vs ranking focus**: tricks that sharpen ranking usually damage calibration. Recalibrate (Platt scaling or isotonic regression on held-out data) or don't use them.
- **Dollar-aligned training vs stability**: amount weighting matches TCF better, but one $10k fraud can swing the model.

### Failure modes

- **Downsampling without correction**: the model says p = 0.04 when the true rate is ~0.2%, and the policy over-acts on thousands of good orders. Silent, and common.
- **Class weights silently breaking calibration**: same effect, harder to spot.
- **Broad label, wrong behavior**: training on all chargebacks teaches the model that good-looking, established customers are risky (friendly fraud). False declines rise among your *best* customers.
- **Train–evaluate mismatch**: trained on order-count loss, judged on dollar recall. It improves where orders are frequent, not where the dollars are.
- **Fine on average, broken at the top**: overall log loss improves while top-1% ranking gets worse.

### Connections

- **← 1.3**: training loss is L0 at the bottom of the pyramid.
- **← 1.4**: the cost policy requires calibrated p.
- **← 2.2**: the label audit sets the label gap.
- **→ 3.2**: what happens when proxies are pushed hard.
- **→ 4.2 / 4.4**: label definition, and how the policy consumes p.
- **→ Modeling**: loss, sampling and calibration during training.

## 3.2 Goodhart and misalignment

### What problem it solves

Part 3.1 chose a proxy that *correlates* with the goal. **Goodhart's law**: *when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.* A correlation that holds normally can break once you **push hard on the proxy**, and pushing hard is exactly what optimization does: gradient descent, hyperparameter search, threshold tuning, team incentives. This part teaches you to predict *how* a proxy will break before it does.

### The core picture

As optimization pressure increases, the proxy keeps improving while the real goal peaks and then gets *worse*:

<Diagram src="problem-framing/goodhart-divergence.svg" />

Left of the dashed line, the declined orders are mostly fraud, so both curves rise together. Right of it, the extra declines are mostly **good customers**: "fraud caught" keeps climbing while TCF gets worse.

### How it works: four ways proxies break

Each type has a **different fix**:

| Mechanism | What happens | ShopFast example | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Regressional** | Proxy = goal + noise. Selecting the best by proxy also selects lucky noise. | 60 configs tried; the test-set winner gains half as much online. | An untouched final holdout; shrink estimates; confirm with A/B |
| **Extremal** | The link holds in the normal range and breaks at extremes. | Raising the decline rate from 1% to 5% maximizes fraud caught, and TCF collapses. | Pair every proxy with a **counter-metric**; stay where your data covers |
| **Causal** | The proxy correlates with the goal but doesn't *cause* it. | Risk is measured on *chargeback rate*, so they auto-refund disputed orders before they become chargebacks. Rate −30%; money lost unchanged. | Measure the goal itself (dollars lost) |
| **Adversarial** | Another party optimizes against your proxy. | The model trusts "account older than 30 days"; fraudsters age accounts or buy aged ones. | Features **costly to fake**; monitor where fraud shows up; the fast rules layer (2.1) |

A fifth, not about the model: **organizational misalignment.** If Risk's bonus is tied to fraud losses alone, they will over-block (asymmetric visibility again, 1.2). Incentives *are* an objective function, and people optimize them too.

### Assumptions that Goodhart breaks

- **The correlation survives intervention.** Causal breaks this.
- **Nobody is gaming it.** Adversarial breaks this.
- **You stay in familiar territory.** Extremal breaks this.
- **Selection doesn't amplify noise.** Regressional breaks this.

### Design choices

1. **Metric pairs**: every optimized metric gets a counter-metric with veto power (1.3 guardrails).
2. **Limit optimization pressure**: cap search budgets, keep a final holdout nobody tunes on, change thresholds in small steps with measurement in between.
3. **Feature robustness**: prefer signals expensive to fake (months of device history, behavioral patterns) over cheap ones (account age, email domain).
4. **Periodic re-validation**: re-check the proxy→goal link each quarter with matured labels and A/B results.

### Trade-offs

- **Robust vs strong features**: costly-to-fake signals are often weaker today; cheap signals are strong until attackers notice.
- **Explainability vs gaming**: explaining declines (5.3) also teaches fraudsters what to change. Explain in detail to regulators and support; coarsely to customers.
- **Guardrails vs speed**: every counter-metric slows shipping. That's the point, but too many and nothing ships (1.3).

### Failure modes

The four mechanisms in practice: a model that "wins" offline and underwhelms online; threshold creep; metrics that improve while the money doesn't; fraud migrating into trusted segments. Plus **incentive-driven over-blocking**.

The meta-failure: **only watching the proxy**. If nobody looks at TCF, every one of these stays invisible.

### Connections

- **← 1.2**: incentives and asymmetric visibility are organizational Goodhart.
- **← 1.3**: counter-metrics and guardrails are the main defense.
- **← 3.1**: this stress-tests the proxy chosen there.
- **→ 3.3**: multiple objectives, formalized.
- **→ 5.3 / 6.3 / 6.4**: explainability vs gaming, feedback loops, adversaries.
- **→ Evaluation**: winner's curse and holdout discipline.

## 3.3 Multiple objectives

### What problem it solves

ShopFast doesn't have one objective. It has at least six, and they conflict:

| Objective | Direction | Conflicts with |
|---|---|---|
| Fraud loss | ↓ | False declines, friction |
| False-decline cost | ↓ | Fraud loss |
| Step-up friction | ↓ | Fraud loss |
| Review queue | ≤ 2,000/day | Fraud loss (review is your cheapest good action, per 1.4) |
| Segment fairness | Decline ratio ≤ 2× baseline | Pure TCF |
| Latency | p99 < 100 ms | Richer features / bigger models |

You need a **principled way to combine them**. Otherwise the combination happens implicitly, in whoever picks the threshold on a Friday afternoon.

### How it works: four combination methods

**1. Scalarize (weighted sum).** Convert everything into one number. **TCF is exactly this**: every term in dollars, weight 1.
- ✅ Decisive, and one number to optimize.
- ❌ The weights are value judgments ("a false decline costs $20 of future value"), and some things can't be priced honestly (fairness, legal).

**2. Constrain.** Optimize one objective, subject to limits on the others:

> minimize TCF, subject to: queue ≤ 2,000/day · approval ≥ baseline − 0.2pp · segment decline ratio ≤ 2× · p99 < 100 ms

This is what 1.3's guardrails formalize. The hidden bonus is the **shadow price**: how much TCF would drop if you relaxed a constraint by one unit. It turns "should we hire reviewers?" into arithmetic (see the exercise).

**3. Pareto frontier.** When stakeholders can't agree on weights, show them the **set of best achievable trade-offs** and let them pick:

<Diagram src="problem-framing/pareto-frontier-fraud-vs-false-declines.svg" />

How to read it:
- **Points on the curve** are the best achievable trade-offs, meaning model thresholds that can't reduce one cost without increasing the other.
- **Current rules sit inside the frontier** (dominated). A point exists that is **better on both axes**. Moving there is a free improvement; nobody has to lose. Always check for this first.
- **The dashed line** marks every combination with the same TCF. Where it just touches the frontier is the TCF minimum. **Change the weights** (e.g., value false declines at lifetime value) and the line tilts, so the minimum slides along the curve.
- **Below the curve** is impossible with this model. Getting there needs *better signal* (2.2), not better tuning.

**4. Priority order.** Hard requirements first, then optimize: legal and compliance rules are absolute (the hard-rules slot in 2.1), and TCF is optimized within what's left.

**Most real systems combine methods**: priority order for the law, constraints for guardrails, scalarized TCF inside those, and the Pareto view when stakeholders need to renegotiate weights.

### Where to combine: model or policy?

- **Model level (multi-task)**: separate heads for stolen card, account takeover and friendly fraud, trained with a combined loss. Better features are shared, but **changing the business weights means retraining**.
- **Policy level** (the ShopFast default): the model outputs separate calibrated probabilities, and the policy layer combines them with costs and constraints. **Weights change without retraining.** This is the 1.4 principle again: keep business logic out of the weights.

### Assumptions

- Every objective can be **measured**. False declines are hard to measure (6.2).
- The frontier estimated offline is **real**. Selection bias can make it optimistic.
- Stakeholders will **commit** to weights or constraints and revisit them on a schedule, not constantly.

### Design choices

1. **Scalarize vs constrain vs Pareto**, per objective. Price what's priceable; constrain what isn't.
2. **How tight the constraints are**: every constraint has a shadow price, and you should compute it.
3. **Where the combination lives**: model or policy (above).
4. **Review cadence**: weights and constraints get revisited quarterly with matured labels.

### Trade-offs

- **Scalar**: decisive, but hides the disagreement inside a coefficient.
- **Constraints**: protect stakeholders, but can quietly cost millions. The shadow price makes that visible.
- **Pareto**: transparent, but needs a decision-maker with authority (1.2 decision rights).
- **Combining in the model vs in the policy**: shared representations vs flexibility.

### Failure modes

- **Hidden weights**: nobody wrote down the trade-off, but a threshold choice implies one. Nobody can defend or revisit it.
- **Staying dominated**: the team debates *which* trade-off to take while sitting inside the frontier, where free gains are available.
- **Infeasible constraint set**: no threshold satisfies every guardrail at once. You find out at launch, not in design. Check feasibility on historical data early.
- **Stale weights**: set once, while costs changed (new fees, new liability rules).
- **Fairness treated as a pure price**: some constraints are ethical or legal commitments, not dollar trade-offs. Showing the shadow price informs the decision; it doesn't make it for you.

### Connections

- **← 1.2**: TCF is the scalarization, and decision rights resolve Pareto choices.
- **← 1.3**: the guardrails *are* the constraints.
- **← 1.4**: the cost matrix supplies the weights.
- **← 3.2**: counter-metrics are two-objective constraints against Goodhart.
- **→ 3.4**: time adds another objective axis: now vs later.
- **→ 4.4**: the policy layer implements the combination.
- **→ 5.4**: fairness constraints in depth.

## 3.4 Short vs long term

### What problem it solves

Every metric so far has been measured over **weeks to a quarter**. But many effects of a fraud policy play out over **months to years**, and standard A/B tests never see them:
- A falsely declined customer doesn't just lose one order. They may never come back.
- Strict blocking today pushes fraud rings toward softer targets. Loose blocking gets ShopFast a reputation among fraudsters as an easy mark.
- Declining more today means fewer labels tomorrow, so the model slowly loses sight of what it declines (6.2).

A policy can **win every quarterly A/B test and still lose money over two years.** This is Goodhart (3.2) applied to time.

### How it works: map effects by time horizon

For any policy change, sort its effects into a grid. Here it is for "tighten the fraud policy":

<Diagram src="problem-framing/tightening-policy-short-vs-long-term-effects.svg" />

Both long-term cells can dominate. Which one wins is **an empirical question**, and you need specific tools to answer it.

**ShopFast's long-term effects, in full:**

| Effect | Direction when tightening | Time scale |
|---|---|---|
| Customer churn after false decline | Cost | 3–12 months |
| Step-up fatigue (3DS on every order, abandonment creeps up) | Cost | Months |
| Deterrence (fraud rings share soft targets with each other) | Benefit | Months |
| Label starvation (declined orders never get labels) | Cost | Model versions |
| Card-network standing (months above the threshold means fines and higher fees) | Benefit | Quarters |
| Seller trust (if sellers bear losses) | Benefit | Quarters |

### Tools for measuring long-term effects

1. **Long-term holdout.** Keep 1–2% of customers on the old policy for 6–12 months and compare cumulative spend, retention and fraud. This is the only direct measurement. It's slow and costs a little, and it's worth it.
2. **Surrogate index.** Find short-term signals that *predict* the long-term outcome (e.g., "30-day return rate after a false decline" predicts 12-month spend). Fit that relationship once using the long-term holdout, then use the surrogate in routine A/B tests. It's fast, but it is itself a proxy, so re-validate it (3.2).
3. **Put the long term into the cost matrix.** The "$20 expected lost future value" in 1.4 is exactly this. The holdout is how you *measure* it rather than guess it.
4. **Friction budgets.** A per-customer cap on friction, e.g., "trusted customers see at most 1 step-up per 30 days." It's a guardrail against effects that accumulate over time.
5. **An explicit horizon in the success spec (1.2).** Write down "evaluated at 120 days and re-confirmed at 12 months," so nobody declares victory on week-3 numbers.

### Assumptions

- Long-term effects are **measurable** within a horizon the business will wait for.
- The environment is **stable enough** that a 12-month holdout still means something when it completes.
- Holdout customers **don't mix** with treatment customers (shared devices, households).

### Design choices

1. **Horizon**: a quarter vs a year. Finance often wants quarters; customer value lives in years. Pick one explicitly.
2. **Holdout size and duration**: bigger and longer means more precise and more expensive.
3. **Surrogate choice**: which short-term signals stand in for long-term value, and how often they get re-validated.
4. **Discount rate**: how much a dollar in 12 months counts versus a dollar today.

### Trade-offs

- **Speed vs truth**: weekly A/B iteration vs the months long-term effects need. Surrogates bridge the gap at some risk.
- **Holdout cost vs knowledge**: holdout users get a worse (or just different) policy. **Fraudsters must not be able to tell who is in the holdout**, or they'll target it. Randomize by customer and keep the assignment secret.
- **Deterrence vs churn**: both are long-term and both are uncertain. Tighten too much and you lose good customers; loosen too much and you become a target.

### Failure modes

- **Death by a thousand cuts**: ten A/B wins in a row, each adding a small step-up. Nobody sees the cumulative friction, and abandonment creeps up across the year.
- **Holiday loosening**: the policy is loosened for Black Friday conversion. Fraud rings notice, and attack volume stays high for months afterwards.
- **Stale LTV term**: the $20 estimate is from two years ago. Customer value has changed, and so have the correct thresholds.
- **Contaminated holdout**: shared devices and family accounts cross arms, so the measured difference shrinks toward zero.
- **Horizon mismatch between teams**: Risk optimizes the quarter, Growth optimizes the year, and each blames the other.

### Connections

- **← 1.2**: the horizon and the margin-vs-lifetime-value choice come from the success spec.
- **← 1.4**: the long-term cost term lives in the cost matrix.
- **← 3.2**: this is Goodhart along the time axis.
- **← 3.3**: time is one more objective to trade off.
- **→ 6.2 / 6.3**: label starvation and feedback loops are long-term effects of the policy.
- **→ 6.4**: deterrence and adaptation are adversary dynamics.
- **→ Stage 6**: long-term holdouts and surrogate indexes are evaluation tools.
