## 3.1 Covariate shift

### The problem it solves
If `P(y|x)` truly stays fixed, a *perfect* model wouldn't care that inputs moved. Real models aren't perfect. They're accurate **where training data was dense** and they **extrapolate** where it was thin. Covariate shift pushes traffic into those thin regions.

<Diagram src="monitoring/covariate-shift-into-sparse-region.svg" />

The true curve (dashed) didn't move. That's what makes this covariate shift. But production traffic now sits largely in the region where the model saw few examples, so its **estimate** of that curve is poor there.

### Why it hurts: three mechanisms
1. **Extrapolation error**: the model is least reliable exactly where traffic now concentrates. Tree models (PayCo's GBDT) predict a **flat constant** beyond the edge of the training data. They don't extrapolate trends at all.
2. **Mix shift**: even if accuracy for each value of x is unchanged, **aggregate** metrics change because the mix changed. More high-amount traffic means a higher block rate and different precision, and a threshold that was tuned for the old mix is no longer optimal.
3. **Early warning**: covariate shift is often the first visible sign of something worse, such as a data-quality bug (2.2) or a concept change arriving with new traffic (3.2).

### PayCo causes
- **Planned**: new market launch, a new payment method (e.g. a wallet), marketing campaigns that bring in a different user base
- **Seasonal**: holiday shopping, travel season (more foreign transactions)
- **External**: a big merchant joins or leaves, economic changes
- **Fake**: a data-quality bug that *looks* like shift, e.g. the cents bug. **Always rule out 2.2 first.**

### How to detect it (preview of branch 4)
1. **Univariate, per feature**: compare each feature's distribution with the reference (PSI, KS test). Simple and explainable. Misses **joint** shifts.
2. **Multivariate: domain classifier** (the most useful technique here).
   - Label reference rows `0` and current rows `1`. Train a classifier to tell them apart.
   - **AUC ≈ 0.5** → the two are indistinguishable, no shift. **AUC ≫ 0.5** → shift.
   - Its **feature importances** tell you *what* shifted, and its predicted probabilities tell you *which rows* are unlike training.
   - It catches joint novelty that univariate tests miss: amount is normal, device is normal, country is normal, but a **high amount + new device + foreign country** combination never appeared in training.
3. **Weight by importance**: a shift in a feature the model uses heavily matters more. Combine with output monitoring (2.3).

### The important question: does it actually hurt?
Most covariate shift is **harmless**. Before acting, estimate impact **without labels**:
- **Output check (2.3)**: did scores or decisions move? If not → top-right cell, low priority.
- **Support check**: are the shifted rows inside training coverage? Use the domain classifier's probability as a novelty score for each row.
- **Importance-weighted evaluation**: reweight your labeled validation set by `w(x) = p_prod(x) / p_train(x)` (which you can get from the domain classifier: `w = p/(1−p)`). That gives you an **estimate of production accuracy** without new labels.
  - This only works if `P(y|x)` truly hasn't changed **and** there's overlap. For inputs that never appeared in training, no amount of reweighting helps.

### Responses
| Situation | Response |
|---|---|
| Harmless shift (outputs stable, good support) | Log it, maybe update the reference. Don't retrain |
| Mix shift moved decision rates | **Retune the threshold** (maybe per segment) |
| Traffic in thin regions | **Collect labels there** (targeted manual review), then retrain with recent data |
| Big persistent shift | Retrain, possibly **importance-weighted** toward the new distribution |
| It's really a data-quality bug | **Fix the pipeline.** Never retrain on it |

### Assumptions
1. **`P(y|x)` is fixed.** This is the definition of covariate shift, and **you can't verify it without labels**. You're betting on it.
2. **Overlap**: production inputs fall within the range training covered.
3. Your features **capture** the shift. If the thing that changed isn't a feature, it's invisible here.

### Trade-offs
- **Univariate vs. multivariate**: univariate is explainable but blind to combinations. The domain classifier catches combinations but needs care, and when it says "AUC 0.71" you still have to find out *why*.
- **Sensitivity vs. fatigue**: at PayCo's volume, almost every feature is *statistically* different every day (branch 4 will explain why). Statistical significance is not the same as practical impact.
- **Retrain on recent data vs. keep history**: recent data adapts to the new mix but can **forget rare old fraud patterns** that haven't shown up lately.

### Failure modes
- **Alerting on every shift**: most are harmless, the team stops reading, and misses the real one.
- **Treating a data-quality bug as covariate shift** → retraining bakes the bug into the weights.
- **Univariate only**: joint novelty goes unnoticed.
- **Assuming shift is harmless because `P(y|x)` "should" be fixed**: new markets often bring **new fraud behavior** too, which is concept drift, part 3.2.
- **Forgetting mix shift**: accuracy for each x is fine, but block rate and precision moved and nobody retuned the threshold.

### Connections
- **1.2 / 1.3**: which reference and which window you compare against decides what counts as a shift.
- **2.2**: rule out data-quality problems first. **2.3**: the output check is the relevance filter.
- **Branch 4**: PSI, KS and domain classifiers in depth, plus the problem of statistical vs. practical significance.
- **Branch 5**: targeted labeling in the new region.
- **Branch 7**: retraining strategy and threshold retuning.

## 3.2 Concept drift

### The problem it solves
Concept drift means **`P(y | x)` changes**. The same input now means something different. The model's learned mapping is wrong **even for inputs it has seen thousands of times**. It's the most dangerous kind of drift for two reasons:
- **Inputs can look identical** → input monitors stay green.
- **Scores can look identical** → output monitors stay green.

This is the **bottom-right cell** of the 2.3 matrix: nothing looks wrong, and the model is wrong.

In fraud there's an extra reason it's constant: **adversaries**. Fraudsters probe the model, learn what gets through, and move there. **The model's success causes the drift.** Assume concept drift is ongoing, not an occasional event.

### Four temporal patterns

<Diagram src="monitoring/concept-drift-temporal-patterns.svg" />

| Pattern | PayCo example | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Sudden** | A new card-testing tool bypasses velocity limits. A regulation (e.g. mandatory 3DS) changes who attempts fraud | Needs **fast** detection and a fast patch (rules) |
| **Gradual** | A fraud ring slowly moves from electronics to gift cards | Early labels are a noisy mix. Hard to call early |
| **Incremental** | Fraudsters slowly learn to mimic normal velocity | No single moment to detect. Watch long-window trends |
| **Recurring** | Holiday fraud patterns return every December | **Don't forget them.** Keep seasonal data and don't train only on recent windows |

### How to detect it: you need labels or something close to them
Because `x` and scores can look normal, detection has to use **outcomes**. Ordered from slowest/truest to fastest/noisiest:

1. **Matured-label performance by cohort**: precision/recall/dollar-recall per transaction week (1.3). The truth, but PayCo sees it **30–90 days late**.
2. **Early-label proxies** (branch 5 goes deeper):
   - **Manual review outcomes**: hours to days
   - **Customer fraud reports / dispute initiation**: days, before the chargeback is finalized
   - **Issuer fraud alerts** (TC40/SAFE-style feeds): days
3. **Calibration drift**: predicted fraud rate vs. observed rate **per segment**, using whatever labels have arrived and adjusting for maturity. If a segment's model predicted 0.1% and early reports already show 0.3%, investigate.
4. **Streaming error-rate detectors** (DDM, EDDM, ADWIN, Page-Hinkley): track the error rate as labels arrive and alarm on a statistically significant rise. Useful when labels are fast, as in manual review streams.
5. **Challenger disagreement**: train a model on the **most recent** matured data and score today's traffic with both it and production. If they disagree much more than two models on the same concept normally would, **the concept moved**.
6. **Indirect hints**: volume anomalies in small segments (gift-card purchases under $50 at 12 merchants up 4×). The fraud itself is invisible, but the attacker's **activity** sometimes isn't. This is covariate shift in a niche that points to concept drift.

**Key point: concept drift is usually local.** Adversaries target niches, so aggregate performance barely moves. Monitor segments wherever labels are dense enough, and prioritize high-dollar segments.

### A trap: label-definition drift
Sometimes **the label changes, not the world**:
- An issuer changes its chargeback policy, and more "friendly fraud" (a customer disputing a real purchase) gets recorded as fraud.
- PayCo's ops team changes what analysts mark as fraud in manual review.

This looks exactly like concept drift in the metrics. Retraining on it teaches the model a **new definition**, which may be right or wrong. Always check **label-process changes** (policy, reason codes, labeling guidelines) before blaming the world.

### Responses
| Response | Speed | When |
|---|---|---|
| **Rules patch** (block pattern, cap amount, force step-up) | Hours | Immediate containment of a sudden attack |
| **Threshold tightening** for the affected segment | Hours | While a better model is in progress |
| **Retrain on recent data** (windowed or recency-weighted) | Days | The new pattern **is separable with existing features** |
| **New features** | Weeks | The new fraud **isn't separable** with current features. Retraining alone won't help |
| **Online / continual learning** | Continuous | Very fast drift, with fast reliable labels. Risky (poisoning, feedback) |

The **new-features** row is important: retraining only helps if the features can tell the new fraud apart from normal traffic. If a fraud ring uses fresh cards with normal velocity and normal amounts, no amount of retraining on the same 200 features will find it. You need a new signal, such as merchant-level velocity, device-graph links, or BIN-range patterns.

### Assumptions
1. **Labels eventually arrive and are correct.** Friendly fraud, missing labels on blocked transactions (1.1), and review errors all violate this.
2. **The label definition is stable.** See the trap above.
3. **Some part of the drift is visible in time to matter.** For slow labels and fast adversaries, you may only see it afterwards. That's why fast rules exist.

### Trade-offs
- **Proxy speed vs. truth**: manual review gives labels in hours, but it's biased (analysts only see what's flagged) and small.
- **Retrain frequency**: frequent means fresh, but costly, risks forgetting recurring patterns, and gives adversaries a fast feedback loop to exploit. Infrequent means stale.
- **Detector sensitivity**: an ADWIN tuned tight catches small changes and false-alarms on label-arrival noise.
- **Recency weighting vs. memory**: weight recent data heavily and the December patterns are forgotten by next December.

### Failure modes
- **Relying only on matured labels** → 90 days blind, and the loss has already happened.
- **Aggregate-only performance** → the niche attack is hidden in the average.
- **Retraining when features can't separate the new fraud** → nothing improves, and you conclude "the model is fine".
- **Label-definition change mistaken for concept drift** → the model learns the wrong target.
- **Forgetting recurring patterns** by training only on the last N weeks.
- **Reacting only with retrains** → days of loss while a rules patch could have contained it in hours.

### Connections
- **2.3**: the bottom-right cell, i.e. concept drift is exactly what output monitoring can't catch.
- **3.1**: covariate shift in a niche can be the visible trace of concept drift.
- **Branch 4**: streaming error detectors and calibration tests.
- **Branch 5**: label delay, proxies, feedback loops, label quality. That's where the tools against concept drift live.
- **Branch 7**: rules patches, threshold changes, retraining strategy, new features.
- **Stage 4 Features**: robust, hard-to-manipulate features are the long-term defense against adversarial drift.

## 3.3 Label shift (prior shift)

### The problem it solves
**`P(y)` changes, but `P(x | y)` stays the same.** Fraud looks the same as before. There's just **more** (or less) of it.

PayCo example: a breach dump of stolen cards appears. Fraud attempts double for two weeks. Each fraudulent transaction looks like previous card-testing fraud, and legitimate traffic is unchanged.

It seems harmless ("same fraud, model knows it"), but it breaks three things:
- **Calibration**: by Bayes, `P(y|x) = P(x|y)·P(y) / P(x)`. When the prior `P(y)` changes, the **true posterior changes too**, in a structured way. The model's scores are now **miscalibrated**.
- **Precision at a fixed threshold**: precision depends on the base rate.
- **The optimal threshold**: the cost trade-off from 2.4 depends on how likely fraud is.

What it does **not** break: **AUC and recall**. Ranking is invariant to the prior. This is why "our AUC is fine" doesn't mean the system is fine.

<Diagram src="monitoring/label-shift-class-conditional-scores.svg" />

### PayCo numbers
Per 1M transactions, model recall at the threshold is 70% and the false-positive rate on legitimate traffic is 0.73%:

| | Fraud prior 0.1% | Fraud prior 0.2% (wave) |
|---|---|---|
| Fraud / legit | 1,000 / 999,000 | 2,000 / 998,000 |
| Caught (TP) | 700 | 1,400 |
| Good customers blocked (FP) | 7,293 | 7,285 |
| **Block rate** | 0.80% | **0.87%** |
| **Precision** | 8.8% | **16.1%** |
| Recall / AUC | 70% / same | 70% / same |

Two lessons:
1. **Fraud doubled, and the block rate moved only 0.07 pp.** Fraud is a tiny share of volume, so aggregate outputs barely notice. You have to watch the **top score tail** (2.3). Mass above 0.95 is mostly fraud and roughly **doubles**.
2. **Precision nearly doubled while the model didn't change.** The reverse happens too: when fraud falls, precision falls, and people conclude "the model got worse". **Precision is not a model property. It depends on the base rate.**

### How to detect it
- **Labels (slow)**: the fraud rate per matured cohort.
- **Top-tail output mass (fast)**: share of scores above a high cutoff, compared with the reference.
- **Prior estimation from outputs, without labels**: if `P(x|y)` is stable, a change in the output distribution can be **explained by** a prior change and solved for:
  - **BBSE** (Black Box Shift Estimation): take the confusion matrix `C` from validation and the predicted-class rates `q` in production. Solve `q = C·π_new` for the new prior `π_new`.
  - **EM prior re-estimation** (Saerens et al.): iteratively adjust the prior until the average adjusted posterior matches it.
  - Both depend on the assumption that `P(x|y)` is fixed. If concept drift is happening too, the estimate is wrong.

### Responses: usually cheap
Since `P(x|y)` is unchanged, **you don't need to retrain**. You need to **correct probabilities**:

**Prior correction** for a calibrated model trained at prior `π_old`, deployed at `π_new`:
```
odds_new = odds_model × [π_new / (1 − π_new)] / [π_old / (1 − π_old)]
p_new    = odds_new / (1 + odds_new)
```

**Link to 1.2:** PayCo downsampled legitimate traffic 10× in training. That's a **built-in label shift**: the model learned at about a 1% fraud prior and is deployed at about 0.1%. Without this correction its raw scores overstate fraud risk by about 10× in odds. This is the same formula.

### The design principle: separate estimating from deciding
- **Model + calibration** → estimates `p = P(fraud | x)`. Correct it when the prior moves.
- **Decision policy** → block if `p × C_fraud > (1 − p) × C_FP`, i.e. threshold `t = C_FP / (C_FP + C_fraud)`.
  - `C_fraud` = amount at risk. `C_FP` = lost margin + churn risk. These costs are **per transaction** (a $2,000 purchase and a $5 purchase shouldn't share a threshold).

If calibration stays correct, **the cost-based threshold stays valid automatically**, and label shift is handled by recalibration alone. A fixed score threshold like `0.9` hides all this and quietly becomes wrong whenever the prior moves.

### Assumptions
1. **`P(x|y)` is fixed.** Rarely exactly true. A fraud wave from a *new* group may bring new patterns too (concept + covariate).
2. **The prior estimate is accurate.** Estimating it from **immature** cohorts **underestimates** fraud, because labels are still arriving (1.3).
3. BBSE/EM need a trustworthy, invertible validation confusion matrix. With rare fraud and small segments, that's fragile.

### Trade-offs
- **Recalibrate often vs. stability**: frequent recalibration follows the prior but chases noise from immature labels. Infrequent recalibration stays miscalibrated for longer.
- **Global vs. per-segment prior**: a fraud wave often hits specific BIN ranges or merchants. A global correction under-corrects there and over-corrects elsewhere.
- **Fixed threshold (simple)** vs. **calibrated + cost policy (correct)**: the second needs calibration monitoring and cost inputs from finance, but it adapts correctly.

### Failure modes
- **Judging the model by precision**: "precision dropped 40%!" when the fraud rate simply fell.
- **Never correcting training resampling**: every downstream consumer of scores (rules, analysts, the implied fraud rate in 2.3) is off by 10×.
- **Retraining for a pure prior shift**: expensive and slow, and the new model is miscalibrated too once the wave ends.
- **Prior estimated from immature cohorts** → systematically too low.
- **Upstream filter change**: a rules team adds a pre-filter that removes easy fraud *before* the model, so the model's prior drops silently and calibration breaks. Nobody on the ML team changed anything.

### Connections
- **1.2**: resampled training data is a label shift you create yourself.
- **2.3**: top-tail mass and the implied fraud rate are the early warning. **2.4**: the cost-based threshold and the metric tree.
- **3.1 / 3.2**: real waves mix all three drift types. BBSE's assumption fails under concept drift.
- **Branch 4**: calibration tests and prior-estimation methods.
- **Branch 5**: label maturity makes observed priors biased.
- **Branch 7**: recalibration as a response, cheaper and faster than retraining.
