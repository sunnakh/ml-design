## 4.1 Statistical tests & distance measures

### The problem it solves
Branches 1–3 said "compare current with reference". Branch 4 is **how**: turning two distributions into a **number**, and that number into a **decision**. There are two families of method, and mixing them up is the most common mistake in drift detection:

| Family | Question it answers | Output | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hypothesis tests** | "Is there *any* difference, or could it be chance?" | p-value | KS test, chi-square, two-proportion z-test |
| **Distance / effect size** | "*How big* is the difference?" | Distance | PSI, Wasserstein, JS divergence, KS statistic D |

### The significance trap (the key lesson of this part)
A p-value depends on **sample size**. With enough data, **every** difference is "significant", including meaningless ones.

PayCo example: the `txn_amount` distribution shifts by a trivial amount (KS D = 0.005, roughly the median moving $38.00 → $38.20). Same shift, different window sizes:

| Window size (per side) | KS p-value | PSI noise floor (10 bins, no real shift) |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | ≈ 1.0 → "no drift" | ≈ 0.018 |
| 100,000 | ≈ 0.16 | ≈ 0.0002 |
| 1,000,000 | ≈ 3×10⁻¹¹ → "drift!" | ≈ 0.00002 |
| 10.8M (1 hour, global) | ≈ 10⁻¹⁰⁸ → "DRIFT!!" | ≈ 0.000002 |

Two failure modes, one at each end:
- **At large n, p-values alarm on everything.** Globally, PayCo would page every hour on every feature.
- **At small n, distances are inflated by noise.** Under no real shift, expected PSI ≈ `(k−1)·(1/n_ref + 1/n_cur)`. For a 1,000-row segment that's a PSI of about 0.018 **from nothing**, and occasionally much more.

→ **Rule**: decide on **effect size against a practical threshold**, and require a **minimum sample size** (or a significance check) to guard against small-n noise.

### How each method works

**Numeric features**
- **KS statistic D** = the maximum gap between the two CDFs. Bounded in [0, 1], needs no binning. Most sensitive around the **middle** of the distribution and weak in the **tails**, which is a problem for fraud, where the action is in the tails.
- **Wasserstein (earth mover's) distance** = the average amount of "movement" needed to turn one distribution into the other, **in the feature's own units**. "Amounts moved $3.10 on average" is **interpretable** and sensitive to tails. Its scale depends on the feature, so thresholds are set per feature (or on standardized values).
- **PSI** (Population Stability Index), the standard in credit and fraud:
  ```
  PSI = Σ_bins (cur_i − ref_i) · ln(cur_i / ref_i)
  ```
  - **Bins come from reference quantiles** (e.g. 10 bins of 10% each). Equal-width bins have the same flaw as in 2.3.
  - Empty bins → `ln(0)`. Add a small epsilon, which slightly changes the value.
  - Rule of thumb: **< 0.1 stable, 0.1–0.25 moderate, > 0.25 major**. It's a *convention* from credit scoring, not a law. Calibrate it for your data (4.2).
  - PSI is a symmetrized KL divergence.

**Categorical features**
- **PSI or JS divergence** over category shares. JS is **symmetric and bounded** in [0, 1] (log base 2), and handles categories that appear on only one side without blowing up. **KL divergence** does blow up there and is asymmetric, so it's rarely used directly.
- **Chi-square test**: same sample-size trap as KS.
- **Unseen-category share**: the % of current traffic in categories *absent* from the reference. Simple, interpretable, and catches mapping bugs (2.2) and new markets (3.1).
- **Max share change** (L∞): "the largest category moved 4 pp". Easy to explain in an incident.

**Rates** (null rate, block rate, default-fill rate)
- **Two-proportion z-test**, or better, **control charts** (Shewhart, CUSUM, EWMA). These come from manufacturing quality control and are built for "is this rate still in its normal band?" over time. CUSUM accumulates small persistent deviations, so it catches **slow drifts** that single-window tests miss.

**Many features jointly / high-dimensional** (embeddings, images, text)
- **Domain classifier** (from 3.1): AUC as the effect size, feature importances for explanation.
- **MMD** (maximum mean discrepancy): a kernel two-sample test on embeddings.
- **Reconstruction error**: fit PCA or an autoencoder on the reference and watch for rising error on current data.

### Hidden assumption: independence
Every p-value assumes **independent samples**. PayCo breaks that constantly:
- A card-testing bot sends 5,000 near-identical transactions. The effective sample size is closer to **1** than to 5,000.
- Consecutive windows overlap (sliding) or are autocorrelated (day-to-day traffic).
→ p-values come out **overconfident**. That's another reason to prefer effect sizes with **empirically calibrated** thresholds (4.2), i.e. thresholds learned from how the metric behaves across historical healthy periods.

### Design choices
| Choice | Recommendation for PayCo |
|---|---|
| Test or distance? | **Distance as the decision metric.** Tests only as a small-n guard |
| Numeric default | **PSI on reference-quantile bins** (the standard, comparable across features) + **Wasserstein** for key features (interpretable $) |
| Tails | Add a separate **tail check** (e.g. share above reference p99) because KS and PSI with 10 bins underweight tails |
| Categorical | **JS + unseen share** |
| Rates | **Control charts** (EWMA/CUSUM) |
| Joint | Weekly **domain classifier** |

### Trade-offs
- **Interpretability vs. generality**: Wasserstein is in dollars, but every feature has its own scale. PSI is comparable across features, but "PSI 0.14" means nothing to a business stakeholder.
- **Binning**: more bins → more sensitive, but a higher noise floor and more empty bins. Fewer bins → stable, but blind inside each bin.
- **Univariate vs. joint**: univariate is cheap and explainable, but misses combinations. Joint catches combinations but needs a follow-up investigation to explain.
- **Compute**: 200 features × 40 segments × several windows. PSI on pre-aggregated histograms is cheap. Domain classifiers and MMD are expensive, so run them less often.

### Failure modes
- **p-value thresholds at scale** → everything alarms, and the team mutes the channel.
- **Fixed PSI 0.1/0.25 cutoffs on small segments** → noise floor alarms.
- **Equal-width bins** on skewed features → tail changes invisible.
- **KL with empty bins** → infinity, dashboards break.
- **Ignoring dependence** (bots, repeated cards) → overconfident tests.
- **Averaging distances across features** → one badly broken feature gets diluted by 199 stable ones.

### Connections
- **1.2 / 1.3**: the reference provides the bins, and the window provides n. Both directly change what any test shows.
- **2.3**: score-distribution monitoring uses the same methods, with quantile/log-odds bins.
- **3.1–3.3**: domain classifier (covariate), error-rate detectors (concept, 3.2), BBSE (label shift).
- **4.2 (next)**: turning distances into alert thresholds without drowning in false alarms (empirical calibration, multiple testing).

## 4.2 Thresholds

### The problem it solves
4.1 produces a **number** (PSI = 0.14). An alert needs a **decision**: fire or not. The threshold is where you trade **false alarms** against **missed incidents**, and at PayCo's scale this trade-off determines whether monitoring gets used or ignored.

Alert fatigue is the reason most ML monitoring projects fail. The problem is rarely missing detectors. It's too many alerts, which people learn to ignore.

### Where thresholds come from

| Source | How | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Convention** | PSI 0.1 / 0.25 everywhere | Zero effort | Ignores each metric's natural noise. Small segments constantly alarm |
| **Business impact** | "Alert if block rate > 1.2%" (the 2.4 guardrail). "Null rate > 1% costs ≈ $X/day" | Meaningful, easy to prioritize | Only possible for metrics whose impact you can model |
| **Empirical (backtested)** | Compute the metric over many **historical healthy windows**. Threshold = p99.9 of that distribution, per feature × segment × window | Automatically absorbs noise floor, dependence, and seasonality | Needs clean history. Healthy periods must be identified |
| **Adaptive / forecast** | Predict the expected value (hour-of-week seasonal baseline, forecasting model). Alert on the **residual** | Handles seasonality and trends | **Can adapt to a slow incident** (boiling frog, like the rolling reference in 1.2) |

**Default for PayCo**: **business thresholds** for the few metrics with clear cost (block rate, approval rate, fallback rate), and **empirical thresholds** for everything else (feature drift distances).

### Anatomy of a good alert rule
A threshold is more than a single number:

<Diagram src="monitoring/alert-threshold-persistence-timeline.svg" />

The parts of a rule:
1. **Value threshold**: the line itself.
2. **Severity tiers**: **warn** (ticket, business hours) vs. **page** (wake someone up). Most drift should warn. Pages are for fast, costly failures.
3. **Persistence (k-of-n)**: fire only if k of the last n windows are over the line. This removes blips but **adds detection delay** (3 hourly windows = up to 3 hours late).
4. **Hysteresis**: fire at 0.25, resolve only below 0.15. Without it, a metric hovering near the line **flaps** (fire, resolve, fire…) and floods the channel.
5. **Minimum sample gate**: don't evaluate if n < N_min. Links to 4.1's noise floor.
6. **Comparable baseline**: the threshold assumes the right reference (seasonal, 1.2) and the right window (1.3).

**Persistence vs. speed depends on the failure type.** For the 100% null-rate break (2.1), use **no persistence**, because one 5-minute window is proof enough. For slow drift, use k-of-n over hours or days.

### The multiple-testing problem
PayCo: 200 features × 40 segments × 24 hourly windows = **192,000 checks per day**. Even if each check has a 0.1% false-alarm rate, that's **192 false alarms a day**. Every check is reasonable on its own, and the total is unusable.

Solutions, from simplest to best:
- **Bonferroni**: divide α by the number of tests. Controls false alarms, but becomes so strict that real drift gets missed.
- **FDR control** (Benjamini–Hochberg): control the **fraction** of alerts that are false (e.g. ≤ 10%), not the chance of any false alert. Much better power.
- **Hierarchy (the best practical fix)**: don't test everything independently. Check **top-level signals first** (outputs, decision rate, fallback rate: a handful of checks). Drill into per-feature and per-segment results **only when a top signal moves**, or only for the ~20 most important features. That cuts the check count by orders of magnitude.
- **Alarm budget**: decide how many false pages a week the team can tolerate (say ≤ 2), and allocate that budget across checks, spending most of it where impact is highest.

### How to know your thresholds work: backtest
Treat the monitoring system itself as a **classifier** of "incident vs. normal":
- **Replay history**: run the rules over the past 6–12 months. Count false alarms in periods known to be healthy.
- **Incident library**: past incidents (the velocity bug, the cents bug, the category-mapping bug). Would each rule have caught them, and **how fast**?
- **Synthetic injection**: deliberately corrupt a copy of the data (multiply amounts by 100, null out 30% of a feature, shift a category's share) and check the detection rate and delay. This is **chaos testing for monitors**.
- The outcome is a **precision/recall/delay** profile per rule, used to tune it the same way you would tune a model.

### Assumptions
1. **History contains healthy periods** you can identify. If last month had an unnoticed bug, empirical thresholds learn it as normal.
2. **The noise process is stable**: traffic volume and mix change slowly enough that last quarter's thresholds still fit.
3. FDR methods assume tests are independent or positively dependent. Drift across correlated features breaks this somewhat.

### Trade-offs
- **Sensitivity vs. fatigue**: the core trade-off. The alarm budget makes it explicit.
- **Persistence vs. delay**: fewer blips, later detection.
- **Per-segment thresholds vs. maintenance**: 8,000 tuned thresholds are accurate, but they rot. Tiered defaults plus tuned thresholds for the top segments are usually enough.
- **Adaptive vs. fixed**: adaptive follows seasonality but can absorb slow incidents. Pair it with a **fixed ceiling** that never adapts.

### Failure modes
- **Copy-pasted 0.25 everywhere**: small segments page nightly, big ones are never sensitive enough.
- **Thresholds set once, never revisited**: traffic doubles, noise shrinks, and the thresholds become too loose.
- **Adaptive baseline absorbs an incident**: the same boiling-frog problem as 1.2.
- **No hysteresis**: flapping alert storms.
- **No minimum sample**: 3 a.m. pages from 40-transaction windows.
- **Never backtested**: nobody knows whether rules would have caught the last three incidents.

### Connections
- **4.1**: the distance metric and its noise floor set what a sensible threshold can be.
- **1.2 / 1.3**: reference and window choice change the metric's baseline behavior.
- **2.4**: business guardrails *are* thresholds, and the most defensible kind.
- **4.3 (next)**: what happens after a threshold fires, i.e. routing, grouping, context, ownership.
- **Branch 8**: thresholds on SLOs and alarm budgets are an operations policy.

## 4.3 Alerting

### The problem it solves
A threshold firing isn't the goal. **A person (or automation) taking the right action quickly** is the goal. Between the two, alerts fail by:
- going to the **wrong team**
- arriving as **one of 40 duplicates** of the same root cause
- saying "PSI 0.31 on feature_117" with **no context**, which leaves on-call digging for an hour

Aim: every alert is **actionable, owned, and understandable in under 5 minutes**.

### How it works: the alert pipeline

<Diagram src="monitoring/alert-pipeline-with-feedback.svg" />

### 1. Group: one root cause, one incident
The cents bug from 2.2 alone triggers: amount PSI in 9 segments, score top-tail up, mobile block rate up, approval rate down. That's **14 alerts for one bug**.
- **Time + label grouping**: alerts within minutes of each other that share a segment or feature → one incident.
- **Dependency-aware suppression** (inhibition): if an **upstream** alert fires (a feature pipeline or data-quality check), suppress the **downstream** alerts it explains (score drift, block rate). This needs a **lineage graph**: source table → feature → model → decision.
- **Risk**: over-grouping hides a *second*, unrelated problem happening at the same time. Suppressed alerts should still be **visible inside** the incident, not deleted.

### 2. Enrich: give on-call context
Most debugging time goes to answering "what changed?". Put the answer in the alert:

```
[PAGE] Block rate spike: mobile channel
  Now 1.9% (ref 0.8%, page threshold 1.2%), since 10:07, 4/4 windows over
  Top segments: mobile/US +1.4pp, mobile/CA +1.2pp; web unchanged
  Top shifted features: txn_amount (PSI 2.8, median $38 -> $3,800)
  Recent changes (last 6h):
    10:00  mobile app v5.2 rollout started (owner: mobile-payments)
    08:15  no model / threshold / rules changes
  Est. impact: ~1,900 extra declines/hour, ~$95k/hour blocked GMV
  Runbook: go/payco-runbook/block-rate-spike
  Dashboard: go/payco-monitoring/mobile   Sample rows: go/payco-samples/INC-4412
```

Essentials:
- **Value vs. threshold vs. reference**, plus since when and how persistent
- **Where**: the top contributing segments
- **Why**: top shifted features or attribution drift (2.3)
- **Change correlation**: model/threshold/rules/config deploys and upstream releases. **Most incidents are caused by a change**, so this line saves the most time.
- **Impact estimate** in business units, which is how priority gets decided
- **Runbook link** (branch 7) and **sample rows** for inspection

### 3. Route: send it to whoever can fix it
| Root cause | Primary owner | Also notified |
|---|---|---|
| Serving latency, fallbacks | Platform / SRE | ML on-call |
| Upstream data (field rename, units) | **Producer team** (mobile, payments) | ML on-call |
| Drift, calibration, model behavior | ML team | Risk |
| Business KPI trend | Risk / product (weekly review) | ML team |

This requires an **ownership registry**: every feature and source table has an owner. Without it, every alert lands in the ML channel, and the ML team becomes a message relay.

**Severity → channel**:
- **P1 page**: fast and costly. Fallback surge, approval cliff, 100% nulls, score collapse.
- **P2 ticket**: act within business hours. Drift warnings, segment calibration issues.
- **Digest**: weekly report. Slow drift trends, KPI movement, low-priority shifts.

### 4. Respond: human, automation, or both
Automate responses that are **well understood and safe to reverse**:
- Canary breaks a guardrail → **auto-rollback** to the previous model
- Feature pipeline fails validation → **auto-quarantine** the batch and keep serving the last good version
- Feature store unreachable → **auto-switch** to the fallback path (it's already built, see 2.1)

Keep humans on **ambiguous** cases, such as drift that might be a real fraud wave. Guard automation itself: rate-limit auto-rollbacks (no rollback loops) and alert whenever automation acts.

### 5. Feedback: monitor the monitoring
- At resolution, label every alert: **true positive, actionable / true positive, not actionable / false positive**.
- Track per rule: **alert precision**, pages per week, **MTTD** (time to detect), **MTTA** (time to acknowledge), **MTTR** (time to resolve).
- Monthly review: **retire or retune** low-precision rules. Add rules for incidents that were found by humans rather than by monitors.
- **Dead-man's switch**: if a detector job dies, "no alerts" looks exactly like "healthy". Send a heartbeat, and alert when it's **missing**.
- **Silences with expiry**: planned launches (Brazil) produce expected drift. Silence the relevant rules **with an end date and a named owner**. Silences left in place indefinitely are a common way real incidents get missed.

### Assumptions
1. **Owners exist and respond**, including upstream teams. That's an organizational agreement, not a technical fact.
2. **Change logs are machine-readable**: deploys, config changes and upstream releases are recorded somewhere an alert can query.
3. **Lineage metadata** exists for dependency-aware grouping.

### Trade-offs
- **Grouping vs. hiding**: fewer notifications, but a real second incident can get buried.
- **Rich context vs. build cost**: enrichment needs lineage, change logs and attribution, which is real engineering work (branch 6).
- **Automation speed vs. risk**: auto-rollback saves minutes and can also roll back a *good* model on a noisy guardrail.
- **Routing upstream vs. control**: producer teams fix things faster, but they need to accept ML alerts in their on-call.

### Failure modes
- Everything goes to one **#ml-alerts** channel that nobody reads.
- Alerts **without runbooks or change context** → the first hour of every incident is spent digging.
- **Alert storms**: 40 pages for one bug, and the real signal is lost.
- **Silences that never expire**.
- **The detector dies silently**, with no heartbeat.
- **No feedback labeling**, so rules never improve, precision decays, and the team learns to ignore pages.
- **Auto-rollback loops** on a flapping guardrail.

### Connections
- **4.2**: thresholds decide *when* to fire, 4.3 decides *what happens next*.
- **2.3 / 3.x**: attribution and segment breakdowns feed enrichment.
- **Branch 6 Architecture**: lineage, change logs, sample storage and alert infrastructure (e.g. Alertmanager/PagerDuty) have to be built.
- **Branch 7 Response**: runbooks, rollback and diagnosis start here.
- **Branch 8 Operations**: on-call rotations, SLOs, and alert review meetings.
