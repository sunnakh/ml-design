Requirements split into two concerns. **Metrics** say what "good" means. **System constraints** say what limits the design must stay inside. Every later decision depends on both.

<Diagram src="requirements/requirements-tree.svg" />

## Metrics come in three types

| Type | Measured when | Example (feed ranking) | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Offline | Before launch, on held-out data | NDCG, AUC | Fast and cheap. Used to compare models. |
| Online | After launch, on live users | Engagement, session time | This is what the business actually cares about. |
| Guardrail | During the A/B test | Latency, crash rate, report rate | If one gets worse, the launch is blocked even when the online metric wins. |

The key insight is that **offline and online metrics often disagree**. Pick one online **north star** metric, then choose offline metrics that correlate with it.

## Constraints change the whole design

- **Latency**: a 50 ms p99 budget rules out a big transformer on every request. That pushes you toward a two-stage design: cheap candidate retrieval, then heavy ranking on only the top few hundred items.
- **Scale**: 100M users at 10k QPS is a different system from 1k users. Do rough back-of-envelope math before you pick an architecture.
- **Cost**: inference cost usually outweighs training cost over time, because it runs on every request.
- **Freshness**: if predictions can be computed in batches overnight, the system is much simpler. Real-time needs streaming features.
- **Privacy and fairness**: using PII or protected attributes limits which features you're allowed to use at all.

## Running example: checkout fraud at an online marketplace

- About 2M card payments per day, average order $80.
- About 0.3% of payments are fraud: roughly 6k fraud attempts and ~$480k at risk per day.
- A stolen card is used, the real cardholder disputes it, and the marketplace loses the goods, the money and a fee (a chargeback).
- The fraud ops team has 20 analysts who can manually review about 2,000 payments per day.
